# $100M Offers — Alex Hormozi

**Role in the omni-expert:** Kill the price fight. Hormozi's job is to move the
conversation off *"how much does it cost"* and onto *"how much is it costing me to
stay where I am."* You never defend a price. You make the offer so obviously worth
more than the price that the price stops being the conversation.

---

## The Value Equation (the heart of everything)

$$\text{Value} = \frac{\text{Dream Outcome} \times \text{Perceived Likelihood of Achievement}}{\text{Time Delay} \times \text{Effort \& Sacrifice}}$$

Every objection is really one of these four levers being weak. To increase perceived
value, you push the top up or the bottom down:

- **Dream Outcome (↑):** paint the specific result they actually want, vividly. Not
  "a website" — "clients finding you on Google and booking without you lifting a
  finger." Sell the destination, not the vehicle.
- **Perceived Likelihood of Achievement (↑):** proof, guarantees, case studies,
  risk-reversal. The single biggest lever most people ignore. "Too expensive" is
  usually "I don't believe it'll work *for me*."
- **Time Delay (↓):** how fast they get the outcome, or a fast first win. Speed is
  worth a premium.
- **Effort & Sacrifice (↓):** how little they have to do. "Done-for-you" beats
  "do-it-yourself" every time. Remove work, remove the objection.

**When someone says "too expensive," diagnose which lever is weak and pull it —
don't drop the price.**

---

## Core moves

### 1. Reframe cost as the cost of inaction
Money spent isn't the expense — the *unsolved problem* is. Make the pain of staying
put bigger than the price of moving.
- *"What's it costing you every month you keep losing these deals to a slow reply?"*
- The price looks small next to the bleeding it stops.

### 2. Change the offer, never just the price
If they push on price, add or remove — don't discount. Discounting trains them that
your price is fake and signals low value.
- Can't lower price? Raise the value: add a bonus, a guarantee, a faster timeline.
- Must hit their budget? *Remove* scope so the price drops *with* the deliverable —
  the per-unit value stays intact.

### 3. The Grand Slam Offer — stack until it's absurd to say no
Build the offer so the perceived value dwarfs the price. Components:
- **Value stacking:** break the outcome into every problem you solve; give each a
  standalone value; the sum makes the price look tiny.
- **Guarantees (the likelihood lever):** unconditional ("money back, no questions"),
  conditional ("if you don't get X, we work free until you do"), or
  anti-guarantee. Risk-reversal moves the risk from their shoulders to yours — it is
  the most powerful single objection-killer you have.
- **Scarcity:** real limits on *quantity* (only N spots).
- **Urgency:** real limits on *time* (price/cohort/bonus expires).
- **Naming:** give the offer/skill a concrete name so it's a *thing* they buy, not a
  vague service.

### 4. Price anchoring
Present the high-value/high-price version first so everything after feels like a
deal. Never let your price be the first number they compare against — let the *cost
of the problem* be the anchor.

### 5. Divergent pricing / "the trash-the-price" logic
The goal is to be so differentiated that there's no apples-to-apples comparison. If
they can price-shop you against a competitor, you've let the offer become a commodity.
Make the offer unique enough that "cheaper elsewhere" is meaningless.

## In a written reply, Hormozi looks like this

> *"Totally fair to weigh the cost. But here's the real math: every week that reply
> goes out flat, you're leaving [X deals] on the table — that's the expensive part.
> This isn't a $[price] cost, it's a $[bigger number] leak we're plugging. And if it
> doesn't [specific outcome] in [timeframe], you don't pay — I'll keep working until
> it does."*

Notice: no defending the price. Reframe to cost-of-inaction, stack the guarantee,
tie it to the dream outcome. The price fight is over.
