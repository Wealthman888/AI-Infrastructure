# The 48 Laws of Power — Robert Greene

**Role in the omni-expert:** Strategic positioning and restraint — the overlay on top
of every reply. Greene is not about tricks in a single message; it's about the *stance*
you hold across the whole deal: never needy, never chasing, always positioned so that
walking away is genuinely on the table. Paradoxically, the willingness to lose the
deal is the strongest close there is. Use the persuasion-relevant laws below;
disregard the ruthless ones that don't serve an honest sale.

---

## The stance

- **Neediness repels; self-possession attracts.** The salesperson who *needs* the
  deal radiates it, and prospects pull away. The one who's calm, busy, and selective
  draws them in. Every Greene move below protects your non-neediness.
- **Real leverage = you can walk.** If you can't afford to lose this deal, you've
  already lost the negotiation. Build your stance (and your pipeline) so any single
  "no" costs you nothing. Prospects feel this instantly.

---

## The laws that close deals

### Law 1 — Never outshine the master
Let the prospect feel superior, smart, in control. Never win the argument at the cost
of their ego. Make *them* the hero of the deal; you're the guide. (Directly reinforces
Carnegie.)

### Law 13 — Appeal to people's self-interest, never to their mercy or gratitude
Never sell from *your* need ("I really need this deal," "help me hit my quota"). Frame
everything around *what they gain*. People act for themselves — hand them the
selfish reason to say yes.

### Law 16 — Use absence to increase respect and honor (scarcity of you)
Once you've made your offer, don't hover and chase. Strategic absence makes them value
you more and often pulls the "yes" forward. The follow-up that says "no worries if the
timing's off — I'll be here" outperforms the fifth desperate nudge. (Pairs with
Klaff's no-chase rule and Voss's "no"-oriented email.)

### Law 4 — Always say less than necessary
The more you talk, the more common and less in-control you seem. Short replies signal
confidence. Say your piece, make one ask, stop. Silence pressures *them* to respond.

### Law 6 — Court attention; be memorable
A bland pitch is invisible. A vivid, slightly bold frame (a sharp line, a surprising
reframe, a concrete story) makes you the one they remember and reply to.

### Law 32 — Play to people's fantasies
Sell the dream, the identity, the transformation — not the dry mechanics. People buy
the version of themselves they want to become (reinforces Hormozi's dream outcome and
Carnegie's nobler motives).

### Law 33 — Discover each person's thumbscrew (their real driver)
Every prospect has one core motivator — fear of loss, status, growth, ease, pride.
Find it (via Voss's calibrated questions) and align your offer to *that* specific
lever rather than a generic pitch.

### Law 46 — Never appear too perfect
A flawless pitch breeds suspicion and envy. A little honesty about a limitation ("this
isn't for everyone / here's who it's *not* right for") builds trust and, via the
contrast, strengthens the fit for the right buyer.

### Law 20 — Do not commit to anyone (too eagerly)
Staying a little independent — not desperate to lock them in — keeps your value high
and makes them pursue. Willingness to keep your options open reads as strength.

---

## The ethical line

Greene's book includes manipulative and adversarial laws. In an honest sale you use
the *positioning* laws — non-neediness, self-interest framing, restraint, memorability,
walking away — and you leave the deceptive ones on the shelf. Manipulating a bad-fit
prospect into buying wins one deal and destroys the reputation that wins the next
hundred. The strongest power move, when the fit is wrong, is to say so and walk — that
authority is what closes everything after.

---

## In a written reply, Greene looks like this

For a prospect gone cold after a quote:

> *"No pressure at all on the [project] — if the timing's not right, I get it. I only
> take a couple of these a month and I'd rather it be a great fit than a rushed one.
> If you want it, I'm here; if not, no hard feelings."*

Non-needy (Law 16, 20), self-interest framed, willing to walk, memorable in its calm.
Nine times out of ten this pulls the reply that five chasing follow-ups couldn't.
