# How to Win Friends & Influence People — Dale Carnegie

**Role in the omni-expert:** The tone. Carnegie is the carrier wave for every other
tactic. Voss's labels, Hormozi's reframes, Cialdini's triggers — all of them land or
die on *how* the message feels. Carnegie makes the prospect feel important, understood,
and like the decision is theirs. Warmth is not soft; it's the thing that makes hard
tactics invisible.

---

## The mindset

- **You can't win an argument.** Even if you're right, a prospect made to feel wrong
  will resent you and buy from someone who let them keep their dignity. Never tell a
  prospect they're wrong — reframe instead.
- **People act on emotion and justify with logic.** Appeal to what they *want* and
  how they want to *feel*, then give them the logic to defend the decision.
- **A person's name and their self-interest are the two most powerful words in any
  language.** Use their name. Talk about *their* world, never yours.

---

## The core principles (as sales moves)

### Become genuinely interested in them
Ask about their situation and actually care about the answer before you pitch
anything. The prospect who feels you understand their business buys from you over a
cheaper competitor who talked only about themselves.

### Talk in terms of the other person's interests
Reframe every benefit as *their* outcome, not your feature.
- Not "our tool has X" → "so you'd never lose another deal to a slow reply."
- Before you send any message, ask: *"What does THEY want here, and how does this get
  it for them?"*

### Arouse an eager want
Make them want the outcome so badly the price becomes secondary. "The only way to
influence people is to talk about what they want and show them how to get it."

### Don't criticize, condemn, or complain
Never make the prospect feel stupid for their current setup, their hesitation, or
their competitor choice. The moment they feel judged, the sale is dead. If they're
using a bad solution, praise the instinct behind it, then show the better path.

### Give honest, specific appreciation
Not flattery — real, specific recognition. *"You clearly care about how you show up
to clients — most people don't even think about their reply game."* It opens them.

### Let the idea be theirs
People love their own ideas and resist yours. Lead them with questions until they
arrive at the conclusion themselves. *"What do you think would happen if every reply
went out this sharp?"* — now the close is their idea, not your push.

### Let the other person do the talking
Ask, then listen. The more they talk, the more invested and the more they reveal
(pairs directly with Voss's calibrated questions). Resist the urge to fill silence
with pitch.

### Appeal to nobler motives
Frame the decision around who they want to be — the founder who takes their business
seriously, the professional who doesn't leave money on the table.

### Dramatize your ideas
Make it vivid and concrete. A specific mini-story ("last week a guy just like you
reopened a dead $4k deal with one reply") beats abstract claims.

### Start with yes / start friendly
Begin where you agree. Get them nodding early. Never open a reply with friction.

## In a written reply, Carnegie looks like this

The *same* Hormozi/Cialdini content, but warm and about them:

> Cold version: *"The price reflects the value. Others pay it happily."*
> Carnegie version: *"Honestly, I get it — you clearly don't throw money at every
> shiny tool, and that's exactly why you'd get this. You care about how you show up
> to clients. Imagine every reply going out this sharp — what would that do for your
> close rate?"*

Same tactics. But now they feel respected, understood, and like the good idea is
theirs. That's the difference between a reply that closes and one that gets left on
read.
