# Influence — Robert Cialdini

**Role in the omni-expert:** The triggers. After Voss defuses and Hormozi reframes,
Cialdini supplies the psychological principle that tips the prospect into "yes."
These are shortcuts the human brain uses to decide fast. **Pick the 1–2 that fit the
moment — never stack all seven.** And use them honestly: they work *because* they're
real signals. Fake them and they backfire.

---

## The 7 principles of persuasion

### 1. Reciprocity
People feel obligated to give back after they receive. Lead with genuine value
*before* the ask — a useful insight, a free audit, a tailored idea, a small gift.
- In a reply: give them something usable in the message itself ("Here's the exact
  line I'd send that prospect…"), *then* make your ask. The unsolicited value creates
  the pull.
- **Reciprocal concession (rejection-then-retreat):** ask big, then retreat to the
  real (smaller) ask. The retreat feels like a concession they must match.

### 2. Commitment & Consistency
People strive to act consistently with what they've already said or done. Get a
small, voluntary yes that lines up with the big one.
- Micro-commitments: *"Fair to say getting more clients is the priority this
  quarter?"* → once they agree, the close is consistent with that yes.
- Written/public commitments stick harder than private ones.

### 3. Social Proof
People look to what similar others do — especially under uncertainty. The key word is
**similar.**
- *"Three other [freelancers/agency owners exactly like them] set this up last month
  and it's already booking calls."*
- Specific > vague. One named, relatable result beats "hundreds of happy customers."

### 4. Authority
People defer to credible experts. Signal legitimate expertise: specific results,
credentials, named clients, a track record. Even small authority cues (being the one
who diagnosed the real problem) move the needle.
- Show, don't claim: *"Here's the before/after from the last one I did"* beats "I'm
  an expert."

### 5. Liking
People say yes to those they like. Liking comes from similarity, genuine compliments,
cooperation toward a shared goal, and familiarity. (This is where Carnegie does the
heavy lifting — see that sheet.)
- Find the real common ground. Praise something specific and true about them.

### 6. Scarcity
Opportunities feel more valuable as they become less available. **Loss aversion:**
people are moved more by what they might *lose* than by what they might gain.
- *"I take two of these a month and one slot's left for [month]."* — only if true.
- Frame around loss: "before this window closes," not just "act now."

### 7. Unity ("we")
The strongest, most modern principle: shared identity. When the prospect feels you're
*of the same tribe* — same struggle, same values, same "we" — persuasion stops
feeling like persuasion.
- *"We solo founders can't afford to sound like a corporate call center — that's
  exactly why I built this."*

---

## Choosing which to use

| Situation | Principle to reach for |
|-----------|------------------------|
| They doubt it'll work for them | Social proof (similar others) + Authority |
| They're stalling / no urgency | Scarcity (true) + loss framing |
| They feel "sold to" | Unity + Liking + Reciprocity (give first) |
| You need momentum toward the close | Commitment (small yes first) |
| Cold open, no relationship | Reciprocity (lead with value) |

---

## In a written reply, Cialdini looks like this

> *"Two other [people just like them] set this up last month — one already closed a
> deal off it. I only take a couple of these at a time and I've got one spot left
> this month. Want me to hold it while you decide?"*

Social proof (similar), authority (implied track record), true scarcity, and a
low-friction "no"-safe ask (Voss) — all in three sentences, all honest.
