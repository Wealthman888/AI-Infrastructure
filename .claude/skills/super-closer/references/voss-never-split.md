# Never Split the Difference — Chris Voss

**Role in the omni-expert:** De-escalation and truth-finding. Voss goes FIRST in
almost every reply. You cannot close a person who feels attacked, rushed, or
unheard. His tools lower the emotional temperature and surface the *real* objection
hiding under the stated one. A hostage negotiator's premise: the stated problem is
almost never the actual problem.

---

## The mindset

- **Negotiation is not argument, it's discovery.** Your goal in the first two lines
  is not to win — it's to make the other person feel *understood*, which is the only
  thing that makes them willing to move.
- **"That's right" beats "You're right."** When the prospect says *"that's right,"*
  you've nailed their reality and they've bought in. *"You're right"* means they want
  you to shut up and go away. Aim your summaries at earning "that's right."
- **Tactical empathy:** demonstrate you understand their feelings and see their point
  of view — *without necessarily agreeing.* Understanding ≠ conceding.

---

## The core tactics

### 1. Labeling
Name the emotion or the objection out loud, tentatively, so they feel seen. It
disarms negatives by dragging them into the open.
- Templates: **"It sounds like…"**, **"It seems like…"**, **"It looks like…"**
- Never "I hear that you…" (makes it about you). Keep it neutral and about them.
- Example: *"It sounds like the price feels steep for where the business is right
  now, and you don't want to bet on something that might not pay off."*
- Then **go silent.** Let the label land. They will elaborate — and reveal the real
  objection.

### 2. Mirroring
Repeat the last 1–3 words of what they said, as a question, then shut up.
- They: *"It's just not the right time."* → You: *"Not the right time?"*
- It triggers them to keep talking and explain, buying you the real reason for free.

### 3. Calibrated "How" and "What" questions
Open questions that start with **How** or **What** put the burden on them and make
them solve your problem for you. Never "Why" (feels like an accusation). Never
yes/no when you want information.
- **"How am I supposed to do that?"** — the gentle no. Makes them see your
  constraints without you refusing.
- **"What about this doesn't work for you?"**
- **"What would need to be true for this to be an easy yes?"**
- **"How would this affect [thing they care about]?"**

### 4. The Accusation Audit
Say the worst things they might be thinking about you/the offer *before they do.* It
defuses the objection by naming it first.
- *"You're probably thinking this is just another tool that'll sit unused. You might
  think switching over is a giant headache. And you may be wondering if the price is
  even worth it."*
- Once you've said it, it loses its power and they often rush to reassure you.

### 5. Get to "No", not "Yes"
People feel safe saying no; "yes" feels like a trap. Ask questions that let them say
"no" while meaning yes.
- Instead of *"Do you have a few minutes to talk?"* → *"Is now a bad time?"*
- The famous re-engagement email for a ghosting prospect:
  **"Have you given up on [fixing this problem]?"** — a well-placed "no" ("No, I
  haven't!") snaps a dead thread back to life.

### 6. Anchoring & the Ackerman price move (when negotiating)
- Let them go first if you can — but if they anchor low, **don't meet in the middle.**
  Splitting the difference means you both lose.
- Ackerman: set your target, open at 65% of it, then 85%, 95%, 100% — with smaller,
  slower increases each round (signals you're near your limit). Use precise,
  odd-looking final numbers ($4,850 not $5,000) — they feel calculated and firm.
- Throw in a small **non-monetary** concession at the end instead of more discount.

### 7. Black Swans
Assume there are 2–3 unknown facts that, if you knew them, would change everything
(they have a competing quote, a board meeting Friday, a personal stake). Your
calibrated questions exist to surface them. The deal-breaker is usually a black swan
you haven't found yet.

---

## In a written reply, Voss looks like this

> **"It sounds like the timing feels off and you're not sure this is worth it right
> now."** *(label — defuse first)*
> **"What would have to change for it to feel like the right call?"** *(calibrated
> question — surface the real objection)*

Two sentences. You've lowered the temperature and handed them the mic. Everything
else in the reply (Hormozi reframe, Cialdini proof) comes *after* this, never before.
